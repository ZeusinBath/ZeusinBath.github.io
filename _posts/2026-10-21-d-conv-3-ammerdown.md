## Difficult conversations: learning more at Ammerdown

*Continuing our series on how to become more skillful with difficult conversations and get the best out of conflict.*

In a vibrant multi-cultural or multi faith environment conflict is an inevitable sign of diversity. We can’t avoid it. We need to make the best of it. 
Conflict means difficult conversations we need to have, but all too often we’re not doing very well. Climate change might be the most existential. Israel/Palestine is perhaps the hardest. In our community we also need to talk about death, poverty and inequality, racism and all the othering “isms”. But we seem unable to have a civil conversation about something as unobjectionable as low traffic neighbourhoods.

Bath IFG decided we need to get better at having difficult conversations. 

We started having small home conversations about topics like the dark side of faith, faith and politics, or faith and sexuality. 

We organised [our own an open session which was simply about having good conversations](https://www.bathifg.org.uk/2026/04/25/conversation.html).  

Bath IFG is a member of the international organistion United Religions Initiative (URI). It organises some excellent “difficult conversations” training. This is greatly brought to life with imaginative online techniques and the internation participation of people from “hot” conflict zones. It has [a whole programme on Peacebuilding and Conflict Transformation](https://www.uri.org/what-we-do/peacebuilding-and-conflict-transformation). 

Meanwhile Bath IFG developed its [own programme delivered as an online course by Leon Dundas](https://www.bathifg.org.uk/2026/07/02/difficult-conversations.html). This was very highly rated by all participants. 

Most recently some of attended a workshop Living Well with Conflict: Dealing with Challenging Conversations Workshop. This was delivered by Carolyn Merry, director of [our excellent local multi faith centre Ammerdown](https://www.ammerdown.org/). 

This session was confidently set in a broader formal academic context. Most delegates initially thought of conflict as something to manage. But is’t better if it’s resolved, and best of all if it succeeds as a way to transformation. Carolyn shared the academic [Speed Leas’ “levels of conflict” model](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Speed-Leas-Conflict-Model-Leas-2012_fig2_358939235). 

According to this, as conflict escalates from a simple problem through disagreement and contest to an intractable situation it loses trust and escalates pain at each stage. Tellingly, each stage has a different goal and requires different language. 
With exercises for active listening, reframing and de-escalation techniques this was a powerful teaching, tested on Carolyn’s long experience in conflict situations.

In conclusion: so there are plenty of ways to learn more about how to use conflict skillfully as a way for growth and deeper understanding. We need to continue to learn them and to be able to share these lessons.
